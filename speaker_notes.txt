 1. THE PROBLEM  (chapter)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Let me frame the problem first.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Statistical classifications, briefly
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Brief context for those outside official statistics. Public statistics rely on
shared classifications — they're what makes aggregated numbers comparable across
regions, sectors, and countries. Without a common code there's no coherent GDP
by industry, no harmonised European business demography.

NACE is the European Union classification of economic activities. Every unit in
our business registers gets a NACE code. It's hierarchical, from broad section
down to fine-grained sub-class. And it's revised roughly every fifteen years to
reflect economic change. That's the transition we're handling now: NACE 2.0 to
NACE 2.1.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 When a classification is revised
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

In normal ML maintenance, what changes is the inputs — you retrain on fresh
labels and the target stays the same. With a classification revision it's
different: the Y itself changes. Old codes are gone or shifted, and fresh labels
in the new classification don't exist yet.

At Insee we have a fastText inspired classifier in production since twenty
twenty-three (powered by the TorchTextClassifiers stack) — two point seven
million NACE 2.0 labels behind it. Our question: how do we leverage that asset
to bootstrap a model in NACE 2.1, without waiting two or three years for a fresh
manually-labelled and expensive corpus?


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Our strategy: shifting the old trainset
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The strategy is here on the diagram.

We use LLMs once, offline, to relabel our legacy NACE 2.0 training corpus into
NACE 2.1.

Then we train a new classifier on the relabelled corpus.

The LLM is not a real-time auto-coder — it's a translation tool used once.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 NACE 2.0 → 2.1 and the multivocal codes
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

NACE 2.1 is being adopted across European NSIs.

The complexity for us lies in the fact that the correspondence between old codes
and new codes can be non bijective.

A majority of codes have a clean one-to-one mapping, but some old codes map to
several new codes — typically two to five candidates, up to thirty-eight in
extreme cases.

With these cases, let's call them multivocal, a simple lookup with a mapping
table alone cannot tell you which one applies to a given business activity in
our old training set — you need the activity description and expert judgement.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 The scale of the problem
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

And these multivocal codes are not rare. In the mapping table they're
twenty-five percent — but weighted by the actual distribution in our training
set, it jumps to fifty-two percent. About one point four million observations we
can't recode easily. Far too many for human experts.

 2. HUMAN AND VIRTUAL ANNOTATION  (chapter)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Quick chapter on annotation.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Human ground truth, machine scaling
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

About twenty-five NACE experts annotated thirty thousand items directly in
NACE 2.1, focused on the multivocal cases. That gives us our ground truth.

Two takeaways from this annotation campaign.
Agreement is really imperfect between experts. If you ask 3 experts to label the
same sample, three-way strict agreement is only sixty-two percent,
two-out-of-three ninety-six.

So, accuracy metrics must be read with a critical eye, or even with leniency.

And the campaign made the expert reasoning more explicit for us, helping us
build a consistent LLM prompt. That's the next chapter.

 3. METHODOLOGY  (chapter)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The pipeline itself.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Rule-Based Augmented Generation as constrained selection
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

First we don't ask the LLM directly to classify into seven hundred and
thirty-two codes.
Seven hundred and thirty-two codes is a too large space for reliable open
generation, the LLM hallucinates very frequently.

Instead: constrained selection is much better.
For each observation we know the legacy NACE 2.0 code. The correspondence table
gives us the admissible NACE 2.1 codes — usually two to five. We ask the LLM to
pick from that short list. Manageable prompt, decisions aligned with official
rules.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 The pipeline
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Let's describe the six-step pipeline.

- Get the activity text + optional precisions,
- get the legacy code,
- look up the candidates from the table,
- inject the official explanatory notes for each candidate,
- ask the LLM to pick, parse the JSON response.

The key idea is in step four. The explanatory notes are the official prose
definitions — with inclusion and exclusion rules and examples. We inject those
into the prompt: same reference material a human expert would consult.

Let's call this Rule-Based Augmented Generation (RBAG), by analogy with RAG.
Same idea but the retriever is not vector search — it's a deterministic lookup
in an official table.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 The prompt structure
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The prompt has two parts. A system prompt — same every time, defines the role.
A user prompt — observation-specific, with the activity, the legacy code, the
candidate codes plus notes, and seven explicit rules.

Output is a structured JSON with four fields.

- nace2.1: the chosen code.
- Codable: was the description sufficient (LLM's self-assessment).
- Confidence: LLM's self-assessed classification confidence, used to filter
  low-confidence cases.
- nace_legacy_valid: a useful side benefit — the model flags suspicious legacy
  codes.

Forcing strict JSON is essential at scale — free-text answers are expensive to
parse reliably.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Multi-model annotation
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We tried not to rely on a single LLM. So, we launch the classification process
with three open-source MoE models in parallel — Qwen3-6-35B (with and without
thinking) and Gemma4-26B.
They differ in size, throughput, and how often they decline when uncertain. The
two Qwen3 runs share the same weights but the thinking variant is more than 10
times slower. Gemma4 sits in between in terms of speed.

Each one is used as an independent virtual annotator. We aggregate the three
predictions via a majority vote to make the final relabelling more accurate.
Same idea as any ensemble: complementary errors cancel out.

 4. INFRASTRUCTURE  (chapter)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A quick word on infrastructure.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Our MLOps stack on the SSPCloud
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SSPCloud is Insee's data science platform, built on the open-source Onyxia
project. Underneath, the whole platform runs on a Kubernetes cluster. The
operational point is that every component of our pipeline is available as a
one-click managed service in every SSPCloud project.

For LLM inference we don't call vLLM directly: we go through the on-premise
llm.lab gateway, which uses OpenWebUI and vLLM under the hood. Langfuse versions
prompt templates. MLflow tracks the classifier training. Argo Workflows
orchestrates the pipeline as a Directed Acyclic Graph. MinIO is the shared S3
storage.

Entirely open-source, containerised, reproducible.

 5. RESULTS  (chapter)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Now the results.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Grading the LLM annotators
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Evaluation is hard. The reference is the thirty thousand annotated cases, which
themselves have human disagreement. We use three metrics. Overall accuracy.
Accuracy on the codable subset, where the LLM itself flagged the prediction as
codable. And LLM-only accuracy, restricted to cases where the true code is in
the candidate list — that isolates the LLM's choice quality from cases where the
legacy code couldn't even reach the right answer in the new classification. A
naive evaluation underestimates the model substantially.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Per-model and ensemble accuracy
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

At level five, each model lands around seventy-five percent overall and
eighty-three to eighty-four on the LLM-only subset. The Qwen3 thinking variant
adds one to two points at about ten times the cost of inference time.

The majority vote wins on accuracy, but it means three full inference passes
including a thinking run, so it comes at a significant cost in compute time.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 A note on self-reported confidence
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We hoped to use the LLM's self-reported confidence to filter low-quality
predictions.

Across all three models, that doesn't work: they are overconfident, correct and
incorrect distributions overlap, and raising the threshold loses coverage faster
than it gains precision. A useful signal would need something else, like
consistency across runs or a separate verifier. Ongoing work.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Reconstructing the training set
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Once happy with the LLM on the benchmark, we apply at scale: one point three
million univocal items by table lookup, about one million multivocal items
through the LLM pipeline, dropping uncodable cases. About two point three
million labels in NACE 2.1, with a distribution close to the original.
Let's call this a semi-synthetic training set — real businesses, real
descriptions, but algorithmic labels.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Performance of the retrained classifier
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The last time we retrained the TorchTextClassifiers model, the semi-synthetic
corpus was still an earlier vintage — the pipeline shown today produces better
labels than what fed that training. Even then, we reached around eighty percent
overall accuracy on a representative NACE 2.1 test set. That's very comparable to
what the previous-generation classifier achieves on its own NACE 2.0 task
(trained on 100% human labels).

That's the main take-away of the talk: semi-synthetic LLM labels can substitute
for manual annotations in the early stages of a classification transition.

 6. AN ALTERNATIVE: PURE RAG  (chapter)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A short detour on an alternative we're exploring in parallel.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Why try a pure RAG approach?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

RBAG has limits worth pointing out. It uses legacy NACE 2.0 labels as input — so
any annotation error in the old system propagates. It needs an official
correspondence table. And it only applies to a revision of an existing
classification.

RAG is worth exploring: no dependency on legacy labels, works when no historical
labels exist, more general for any coding task. And we want an empirical
comparison — is RAG close to RBAG, or much worse?


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 What a RAG pipeline looks like
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

In a pure RAG setup, the deterministic retriever is replaced with a semantic
one. Offline, we build a vector database of the NACE 2.1 explanatory notes. At
inference time, we embed the activity description, retrieve the top-k most
similar codes, inject those candidates and their notes into a prompt that
follows the same skeleton as in RBAG, and let the LLM pick the right one.

Compared to RBAG, the trade-off is not about speed: retrieval is fast on our
infrastructure. It is about operational complexity. We now have an extra
embedding model, a vector database and a retriever to maintain.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 RAG vs RBAG: results
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Running both pipelines under identical conditions, the RAG is about ten points
behind the RBAG (sixty-five overall accuracy for the pure-RAG against
seventy-five percent for the RBAG).

The bulk of that gap comes from the candidate set itself: the legacy mapping and
the table lookup capture the true code ninety percent of the time, the RAG
top-five only eighty-three percent.

We also tested simply increasing the top-k (from five to ten). The candidate set
grows and the truth shows up more often, but the LLM then has more noise to
filter through. Its choice quality drops by roughly the same amount, so overall
accuracy barely moves.

The take-away is that RAG is genuinely viable when no legacy labels exist, but
closing the gap requires a better retriever, through improved embeddings or a
reranker, not simply a wider net.


════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
 7. CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVES  (chapter)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Wrap-up.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Take-aways and what's next
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What we've shown: classification revisions can be handled by treating LLMs as
virtual annotators, constrained by the correspondence table and official
explanatory notes. The classifier trained on the semi-synthetic corpus matches
our previous-generation production model.

It matters because classification revisions are recurring, and our approach
drastically shortens the time needed to produce the manual annotations they
usually require.

A few open issues: inference cost is real and we inherit upstream errors. On the
methodology side we're improving the RAG variant and extending the approach to
other classifications.
